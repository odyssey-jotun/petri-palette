# Petri Palette: my StoryBrand framework

The site follows this script. When the message changes, I change the script first, then the copy.

## The name

A petri dish is where scientists grow cells to study them. A palette is where painters mix color. The name puts the lab bench and the easel side by side, which is the whole project in two words.

## One-liner

Biology class asks students to learn cells they have never seen. I paint those cells, one stage at a time, so students can picture the science and remember it.

## 1. The character and what they want

- **Who:** middle and high school students meeting cell biology for the first time. Second audience: the teachers and parents who want them to stay with science.
- **What they want:** to picture what happens inside a cell well enough to understand it and remember it in class.

## 2. The problem

- **Villain:** abstraction. Interest drops once science courses begin to resemble lecture halls and rote memorization rather than physical experimentation and exploration, and cell biology is where that happens first.
- **External:** science scores are sliding. In 2024, 38% of eighth graders scored below NAEP Basic in science, the highest share since 2011, and the average score fell back to its 2009 level.
- **Internal:** students decide they are not science people. Eighth graders who enjoy science activities fell from 52% in 2019 to 42% in 2024. I think of it as self-impeding: students talk themselves out of a subject before they have seen it.
- **Philosophical:** no student should give up on biology because nobody ever showed them what a cell looks like.

## 3. The guide: me

- **Empathy:** I'm a high school student in the same biology classes, and I paint the parts that are hardest to imagine.
- **Authority:**
  - Biology Best in Class (9th grade) and Genetics Best in Class (11th grade)
  - A painting shown at the Arkansas Museum of Fine Arts (9th grade)
  - Stephens Summer Science Scholar at UAMS: 176 hours researching metastatic melanoma and ER stress, presented at the UAMS undergraduate research symposium
  - Vice president of my school's National Honor Society chapter, where I built and run the peer tutoring program
  - Part of a science art tradition that runs from Robert Hooke's 1665 drawing of cork cells to painters working today (the Artists page)

## 4. The plan

1. **Look at the painting.** Notice what looks crowded, bent, split or stretched before reading a word.
2. **Read the title card.** Each painting has a short card that names the cell or stage and explains it in plain language.
3. **Take the picture back to class.** The next time mitosis or protein synthesis comes up, the image is already there.

## 5. Calls to action

- **Direct:** Walk through the exhibit.
- **Transitional:** Read the research. See the artists who paint cells.
- **Later:** come to the gallery showing, once I have a venue and date.

## 6. Failure: what happens if nothing changes

- Biology turns into a list of words memorized the night before a test.
- Students decide they are not science people and stop signing up for science classes.
- Careers in medicine and research start to feel out of reach.

## 7. Success: what life looks like after

- Students remember each stage of mitosis because they have seen it.
- They walk into biology class with an image for every term.
- They see science as something they can understand and enjoy.

## Transformation

From "I'm not a science person" to "I can see how this works."

## Copy rules

- Everything on the site is in my voice ("I"). My name appears only where I introduce myself.
- Students stay in the third person in the hero and problem section. "You" starts at the plan.
- Problem section: one broad headline, then exactly three icon points in the order external, internal, philosophical.
- Every figure is cited in plain text. No external links anywhere.
- Every call to action is centered, and every section has a photo or a painting.
