Paintings of cells, mitosis and the path from gene to protein

11 paintings in three series. Read each title card beside its painting, the way you would in a museum.

Start with the Goggle Series

The Goggle Series

Three cells whose shape tells a story about health and disease, from cells that will not stop dividing to red blood cells bent out of shape.

Cancer Cells

Goggles 1 · Lily Butler

Cancer cells keep dividing after the signals that stop healthy cells have shut off. Under a microscope they often look irregular, with large, uneven nuclei, and they crowd the healthy tissue around them.

Look for the uneven shapes and oversized nuclei.

Flat Cells

Goggles 2 · Lily Butler

Flat cells, called squamous cells, are thin and shaped like scales. They line surfaces such as the outer layer of skin, the inside of the mouth and the tiny air sacs of the lungs.

Look for the thin, tile-like cells pressed edge to edge.

Sickle Cells

Goggles 3 · Lily Butler

Healthy red blood cells are round, flexible discs. In sickle cell disease, an inherited change in the hemoglobin gene makes red blood cells stiff and curved like a crescent, so they can stick in small blood vessels and block blood flow.

Look for the crescent cells beside the round ones.

Mitosis

One cell becoming two, painted as five stages. Human cells carry 46 chromosomes, so the paintings show only a handful to keep each stage easy to read.

Interphase

Before mitosis · Lily Butler

The cell grows and copies its DNA. The chromosomes are loose, stringy and tangled together, so no single one stands out yet.

Look for the messy, jumbled strands.

Prophase

Stage 1 · Lily Butler

The copied chromosomes coil up tight enough to see, and spindle fibers begin to form at opposite ends of the cell.

Look for the X-shaped chromosomes floating free.

Metaphase

Stage 2 · Lily Butler

The chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell. Each one is two identical sister chromatids joined at the center, with spindle fibers attached from both sides.

Look for the line of chromosomes down the middle.

Anaphase

Stage 3 · Lily Butler

The spindle fibers pull the sister chromatids apart, and each half moves toward an opposite end of the cell.

Look for the chromatids pulling away from each other.

Telophase

Stage 4 · Lily Butler

A new nucleus forms at each end, and the cell pinches in at the middle until it splits into two cells.

Look for the pinch in the middle.

Protein Transcription

How a gene becomes a protein. In the nucleus, a gene's DNA is copied into messenger RNA (transcription). A ribosome then reads that RNA and links amino acids into a protein (translation), and the Golgi apparatus packages the finished protein.

DNA to RNA

Transcription · Lily Butler

Inside the nucleus, the DNA double helix unzips, and one strand is copied into a single strand of messenger RNA. That copy carries the gene's instructions out of the nucleus.

Look for the new strand forming inside the open section of the helix.

The Ribosome

Painting in progress · Lily Butler

A ribosome reads the RNA three letters at a time and links one amino acid for each, folding the chain into a working protein.

The Golgi Apparatus

Packaging · Lily Butler

Newly made proteins pass through the Golgi apparatus, a stack of flattened sacs that sorts and packages them. Small bubbles called vesicles bud off the edges and carry the finished proteins where they need to go.

Look for the stacked sacs and the round vesicles around them.
