Why I paint the inside of the cell

I'm a senior at Episcopal Collegiate School in Little Rock. When I read the research on why students give up on science, it kept pointing to one moment: biology moves inside the cell, nothing can be seen anymore, and class turns into memorizing words.

So I paint that part. My notes for mitosis read like stage directions: stringy and jumbled for interphase, loose and floaty for prophase, bouncy for anaphase, pinched for telophase. The summer before senior year I studied melanoma in a cancer research lab at UAMS, and in ninth grade one of my paintings was shown at the Arkansas Museum of Fine Arts. Petri Palette is where those two halves meet.

How to learn a cell by looking at it first

Three steps turn a vocabulary list into a picture you can remember.

  1. Look at the painting

    Start with the shapes and colors. Notice what looks crowded, bent, split or stretched before you read a single word.

  2. Read the title card

    Every painting has a short card that names the cell or stage and explains what is happening in plain language.

  3. Take the picture back to class

    Next time your biology teacher brings up mitosis or protein synthesis, you will already have an image to hang the terms on.

What changes when students can see the science

When cells stay invisible

  • Biology turns into a list of words to memorize the night before a test.
  • Students decide they are not science people and stop signing up for science classes.
  • Careers in medicine and research start to feel out of reach.

When you can picture them

  • You remember what happens in each stage of mitosis because you have seen it.
  • You walk into biology class with an image for every term.
  • You start to see science as something you can understand and enjoy.
